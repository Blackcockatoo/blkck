A Blue $nake Studio cultural proposition · Victoria, Australia

Old VicState

Not nostalgia. Cultural continuity.

Victoria already has an extraordinary visual and cultural inheritance. The job is not to freeze it in time. The job is to hand it forward — altered, alive and owned by the next generation.

The sound of studio and state

The argument, in one breath

Victoria does not only need systems that function. It needs a culture people recognise as their own.

Old Vic State takes the visual authority, craftsmanship, civic pride and local identity of historic Victoria and translates them into a contemporary Victorian language — public art, music, street culture, schools, digital work, wildlife, typography and community memory.

Not a heritage exercise. A working brief for what a state makes next.

The distinction that matters most

Don't rebuild the past.
Build something worth inheriting.

Old Vic State never says Victoria used to be better. It says Victoria inherited something valuable, and we are responsible for what we add to it.

On the history itself.

Victorian history contains genuine achievement and genuine harm, often in the same decade and sometimes in the same building. A project that only borrows the gold leaf is not continuity — it is decoration.

The land this work is made on is Aboriginal land. Sovereignty was never ceded. Blue $nake Studio works in Frankston, on Bunurong Country.

First Nations culture is not an aesthetic resource. No element of First Nations language, story, symbol or design is available to this project without permission from the relevant community. Any collaboration must be permission-led, local, properly paid and genuinely collaborative — or it does not happen.

The studio's written protocol on this is drafted and public. It has not yet been reviewed by a First Nations advisor, and until that review happens it is not used operationally. That is stated here rather than buried.

The philosophy · five principles

A state should know what it looks like.

Five rules. They are short because they are meant to be used, argued with and applied by people who are not us.

01

Keep what has weight

Architecture, craft, stories, symbols, places, language and community memory should not disappear simply because they are old.

Weight is the test — not age. A bluestone lane, a station canopy, a mill, a hand-painted shopfront, a family's forty-year business, a phrase only used in one town. If losing it would leave a hole, it has weight.

Preservation does not mean paralysis.

02

Create what is missing

Every generation deserves to add something worthy of being inherited by the next. Public culture should not stop at conservation.

Victoria in the 1880s was not being careful. It was building the most confident public architecture in the country and putting ornament on things that did not need it. That nerve is the inheritance — not the ornament.

The people we preserve were not preserving. They were making.

03

Put art back into civic life

Art should exist in schools, streets, libraries, stations, parks, shops, public buildings and everyday infrastructure. Not as decoration. As identity.

The best version of this does a job as well: a mural that teaches, a plaque that plays, a wall that tells you where you are, a bench that carries a local story, a signal box that becomes a landmark.

Public art should do something.

04

Give young Victorians ownership

Children and young people should not merely be audiences for culture designed by adults.

Let them make it. Let them alter it. Let them leave evidence they were here. Ownership means at least one thing in the finished work arrives unchanged from a young person — not consulted, not softened, not redrawn by a professional afterwards.

Consultation is not ownership. Ownership survives the adults leaving the room.

05

Make every place unmistakably itself

Do not roll one aesthetic across Victoria. Ballarat should feel like Ballarat. Frankston should feel like Frankston. Geelong like Geelong. Dandenong like Dandenong.

Bendigo, Shepparton, Morwell, Mildura, Warrnambool and Footscray should exaggerate what makes them themselves.

Local identity is the system. Not the decoration on top of it.

Principle 03, proved

Public art should do something.

Art does not become less artistic because it is useful. Victoria's best old buildings were railway sheds, markets and pumping stations.

The part people forget to count

Healthy technology is civic architecture too.

The last generations left Victorian children stations, libraries, schools, parks, streets and signs. This one is mostly leaving them software.

Interfaces, platforms and behavioural systems are now where children spend their public life. They are built by someone, they shape behaviour, and they will outlast the companies that made them. That puts them squarely inside the question this whole page is asking: what are we leaving behind?

A civic culture that gets the murals right and hands its children extraction-based software has not kept what has weight. It has just decorated the loss.

What they left us

  • Stations, libraries, schools
  • Parks, streets, public buildings
  • Signs, seals, lettering
  • Objects built to outlive their builders

What we are leaving them

  • Software, interfaces, platforms
  • Digital environments and defaults
  • Behavioural systems and incentives
  • Objects built to outlive their builders

Blue $nake Studio's answer is a design test, not a product: no ads, no trackers, no student login, no data taken from a child. Meta-Pet and the studio's browser tools exist as evidence that the test can be met and something good still gets built. They are proof of a philosophy, not the centre of this argument.

The first laboratory

Frankston first.
Not Frankston only.

Blue $nake Studio is based in Frankston. So Frankston is where the thinking gets tested at city scale before it is offered to anyone else.

That work already has a name and a document set: Frankston 2035 — a ten-year civic creativity project covering permanent school artwork, healthy technology in the library, a QR art trail through the CBD and foreshore, curriculum-linked public art, local music and storytelling, and a First Nations protocol that governs all of it.

Old Vic State is what Frankston 2035 is an example of. The studio can speak for Frankston because it lives here, watches here and gets things wrong here. It cannot speak for Bendigo.

So what travels is the method, not the look. How you find what a place already is, who gets to decide it, and how young people end up holding the pen. Nobody needs Frankston's palette.

Claims register

What Old Vic State is not claiming

  • Not a government project. Blue $nake Studio is an independent creative practice in Frankston. Nothing here is government-funded, endorsed or commissioned, and no council, school or library has adopted any of it.
  • Not a political movement or advocacy collateral. No party, no candidate, no campaign, no alignment with any advocacy organisation. It is a cultural argument that anyone is free to use, argue with or ignore.
  • Not a finished program. Most of what this page describes is proposal-stage. The register above says exactly which parts.
  • Not a cultural authority. The studio does not speak for any community, least of all a First Nations one.
  • Not a claim that old Victoria was better. It was not uniformly better. It was uniformly more confident about making things. That is the part worth taking.

Concrete possibilities · none of these exist yet

What this looks like when it works.

Not promises. Eight specific things councils, schools, libraries, artists and businesses could build together — each small enough to actually start.

A school that makes its town's artwork

A regional school producing a permanent civic artwork for its own town — not a school project displayed at school, but a piece of the town made by people who are twelve.

Libraries as production spaces

A library where you can record, draw, animate and print — not only borrow. Cultural production as a public service, on tools that do not harvest children.

Old motifs, contemporary hands

Contemporary artists commissioned to reinterpret Victorian ornament, lettering, ironwork and tile — properly paid, and free to break it.

Musicians scoring places

A local musician commissioned to write for a specific place: a pier, a rail line, a main street, a mill. The place gets a sound as well as a look.

Artists and tradespeople together

Signwriters, welders, bricklayers, stonemasons, printers and glaziers working with artists, historians, schools, councils and businesses. Craft is the missing half of most public art.

Wildlife as civic symbol

Local species as contemporary emblems. Black cockatoos, brolgas, hooded plovers, Leadbeater's possum, the towns that already half-claim them. Living symbols instead of borrowed ones.

Empty infrastructure as canvas

Substations, silos, water tanks, underpasses, hoardings, shutters, blank end-walls. Victoria has enormous quantities of unused public surface and no shortage of people who can paint.

A living archive

A digital archive that grows as Victoria grows: what was made, who made it, where it is, what it replaced. The record is part of the work.

Where this sits

Repair is three moves,
not one.

Naming the damage is not repair. Treating the same damage every year is not repair either. Repair is what happens when the conditions producing the damage change, and something better gets built in the space that opens up.

Most public argument in Victoria stops at the first move and calls it the whole job.

I

Identify the wound

Say plainly what is broken. Necessary, loud, and the easiest of the three to mistake for progress.

II

Change the conditions that caused it

Fix what keeps reproducing the damage — how things get commissioned, who decides, what gets maintained, what children are handed by default. Slow, unglamorous, and where prevention actually lives.

III

Build something better in the space

The part almost nobody funds and everybody misses when it is absent. This is where Old Vic State works.

Fixing Victoria cannot only mean repairing what is broken. Someone also has to imagine what is worth building.

Old Vic State occupies the third space and does not pretend otherwise. But the moves are connected: a mural built without changing who decides, who maintains it and who owns it is a repair that will need repairing. Good civic design prevents the next failure and leaves something worth inheriting. Prevention and creation are the same craft, running in different directions.

The proposition is deliberately built so it can be handed to a council, a school, a cultural institution, a business, a community group or a civic-repair organisation without translation — and without becoming theirs. It stays an independent studio project, unaligned with any political or advocacy organisation. That independence is what makes it worth taking.
